At exactly 11:47 every night, the clocks in Bellweather stopped. Nobody knew why. The first time it happened, people blamed the electricity. The second time, they blamed the clocks. By the third night, they blamed the town. Bellweather was a small town surrounded by green hills, thick pine forests, and a narrow river that disappeared beneath the mountains before eventually finding its way toward the sea. It had one main road, one railway station, three schools, two churches, a library, a hospital, and an enormous clock tower standing proudly in the center of the market square. The clock tower was the oldest structure in the town, built 127 years earlier by the town's first settlers. According to every photograph, newspaper clipping, and surviving record, the clock had never stopped. Until that winter.

The strange thing wasn't simply that the clocks stopped. The strange thing was that every morning at exactly six o'clock, they started again. Always six o'clock. Never six-oh-one. Never five-fifty-nine. Every morning, the people of Bellweather woke up believing they had slept normally. They went to work, opened their shops, sent their children to school, argued with their neighbors, bought groceries, complained about the weather, and continued living their ordinary lives. Nobody remembered anything unusual. Nobody except seventeen-year-old Arun Sen.

Arun lived above his father's repair shop on Market Street. His father repaired watches, radios, old cameras, clocks, and occasionally objects that had no obvious reason for being brought to a repair shop. Arun had inherited his father's habit of taking broken things apart. Unfortunately, he had not inherited his father's ability to put them back together properly. "You've made it worse," his father would often say whenever Arun attempted a repair. "I've made it different," Arun would reply. His father usually shook his head and muttered that "different" was simply another word people used when they had made something worse. Arun liked that explanation. To him, different sounded more interesting than better.

One Tuesday evening in December, Arun was sitting behind the repair counter, struggling with an antique pocket watch, when the clock on the wall suddenly stopped. The second hand froze. Arun looked at the clock. It showed 11:47. He checked his phone. It also showed 11:47. Confused, he looked at the old railway clock hanging above the shop's front door. Its hands were frozen at exactly the same position. Outside, the town was strangely quiet beneath a layer of pale winter fog. Arun waited for the second hand to move, but it didn't. His father walked into the shop carrying two cups of tea and immediately noticed his son's expression. "What's wrong?" he asked. Arun pointed toward the wall. "The clocks stopped." His father looked at the clock, then at the railway clock, then at the phone in Arun's hand. "What time is it?" he asked. "11:47." His father became unusually quiet. Then, after a few seconds, every clock in the shop suddenly started moving again. The wall clock changed to 11:48. Arun looked at his father. His father looked back at him. "Probably the electricity," his father said. Arun frowned. "The pocket watch doesn't use electricity." His father took a sip of tea. "Then probably the pocket watch." Arun pointed toward the railway clock. "What about that one?" His father shrugged. "Probably the railway." Arun held up his phone. "And my phone?" His father stared at him for a moment. "Probably you." Arun laughed, but his father didn't.

The next morning, Arun almost forgot about the incident. He went to school, attended mathematics, fell asleep during history, argued with his friend Kabir about whether a hot dog could technically be considered a sandwich, and spent most of lunch trying to convince their science teacher that the school's old microscope was haunted. By evening, the strange event from the previous night had become nothing more than a curious memory. Then, at 11:47, it happened again.

This time Arun was outside. He was walking home from Kabir's house when the streetlights flickered. One by one, they went dark. Then the entire town became silent. There were no barking dogs, no passing cars, no sound from the river, and not even the usual rustling of trees. It was as if someone had pressed a button and muted the entire world. Arun took out his phone. 11:47. He waited for the lights to return. One minute passed. Then two. Then five. Nothing happened. Something inside him told him to go home, but curiosity was stronger than fear. Instead of walking toward his house, he walked toward the market square.

The clock tower stood above him, enormous and dark against the cloudy sky. Its hands pointed stubbornly toward 11:47. Arun walked around the tower and noticed something he had never paid attention to before: an old wooden door at the back. He had passed that door hundreds of times, but he had never seen it open. Tonight, it was slightly ajar. He pushed it carefully. The door opened with a long, unpleasant groan. Behind it was a narrow staircase leading downward into darkness. Arun switched on his phone flashlight and began descending. He counted the steps as he went. Twenty. Thirty. Forty. Fifty. The staircase seemed impossibly long. At fifty-three, he finally reached a stone corridor. At the end of the corridor stood a heavy metal door. Someone had carved words into its surface.

ARUN SEN.

Arun stopped breathing for a moment. Under his name was another sentence.

YOU ARE EARLY.

He stared at the words, trying to understand who could possibly have known he would come there. Then he heard footsteps behind him. He spun around and raised his phone. A girl stood at the bottom of the staircase. She looked about his age. She wore a dark green coat, muddy boots, and a red scarf wrapped tightly around her neck. In her hand was an old lantern. "You're not supposed to be here," she said. Arun stared at her. "Who are you?" She tilted her head. "I could ask you the same thing." Arun pointed toward himself. "I'm Arun." The girl's expression suddenly changed. She stepped closer and studied his face. "You're him." Arun frowned. "Apparently." The girl shook her head. "I'm Mira." Arun asked if she lived in Bellweather. She replied, "No." He asked where she lived. She looked around the underground corridor. "Here." Arun sighed. "That's not an answer." Mira smiled faintly. "It's the only one I have."

She walked past him and opened the metal door. Beyond it was a room unlike anything Arun had ever seen. The walls were covered with clocks. Hundreds of them. There were small clocks, enormous clocks, pocket watches, digital displays, golden clocks, wooden clocks, broken clocks, clocks without hands, clocks with too many hands, and clocks that seemed to be moving backward. Some were moving so quickly that their hands were nothing more than blurs. Others appeared frozen. In the center of the room stood a massive circular machine made of copper, steel, glass, and countless rotating gears. It looked like someone had combined a grandfather clock with a railway engine and a machine from a science-fiction movie. Transparent tubes carried a strange blue light through the walls. Every few seconds, the machine produced a deep mechanical sound that Arun could feel in his chest.

"What is this?" Arun whispered. Mira looked at the machine. "The reason Bellweather still exists." Arun stared at her. "That's not very reassuring." Mira walked toward an old wooden table. A leather notebook sat on it. "You should read this." Arun picked it up and immediately recognized the handwriting. His grandfather had died before Arun was born, but Arun had seen his handwriting in old letters and photographs many times. The first page contained only one sentence: If you are reading this, then the machine has begun to fail.

Arun turned the page. His grandfather's writing continued. Bellweather was never meant to exist. The town had been built in the wrong place, at the wrong moment, by people who did not understand what they had discovered beneath the mountain. The first settlers had found a cavern where time behaved differently. A stone dropped inside could fall upward. A candle could burn backward. A wound could heal in seconds. A person could enter the cavern and return before they had entered. The settlers believed they had discovered a miracle. Arun's grandfather believed they had discovered a doorway. He eventually realized that both ideas were wrong. The cavern was not a miracle and it was not a doorway. It was a wound in time itself.

The machine beneath the clock tower had been built to keep that wound closed. According to the notebook, it had worked for more than eighty years. But the machine had one terrible flaw. Every night at 11:47, it required energy. That energy came from memories. At first, the machine took small memories from the people of Bellweather: a forgotten conversation, the smell of an old house, the taste of a childhood meal, the face of someone seen only once, the words of a song nobody remembered hearing. Nothing important, or so everyone believed. The machine removed the memory of the event itself, ensuring that nobody noticed what had been taken.

But then something changed.

The machine began taking more than memories.

It began taking possibilities.

Arun looked up from the notebook. "What does that mean?" Mira was standing beside the enormous machine. "Every night, it doesn't only consume something that happened yesterday," she said. "It consumes something that could happen tomorrow." Arun stared at her. "That doesn't make sense." Mira looked around the room. "You're standing underground beneath a clock tower in a room filled with machines that eat memories. At this point, I think we can stop demanding that everything make sense." Arun almost laughed.

Mira pointed toward a calendar hanging on the wall. Arun walked over to it. The current month was December. Today's date was the eighteenth. Every date after the twenty-fourth had been crossed out with thick black ink. "Why December twenty-fourth?" he asked. Mira looked at the machine. "That's the last day the machine can reach." Arun turned toward her. "And then?" She didn't answer immediately. "Then there is no tomorrow."

Arun stared at her, waiting for her to smile or admit that she was joking. She didn't. "You mean metaphorically?" he asked. "No." "Literally?" Mira nodded. "Literally." Arun looked at the calendar again. "So we stop the machine." Mira's face became serious. "There's a problem." "Of course there is." "The machine can't be stopped." "Why?" Mira walked toward the center of the room. "Because it's not actually a machine." Arun frowned. "Then what is it?" Mira placed her hand against the metal casing. "It's a lock."

Arun felt a cold sensation run through him. "A lock for what?" Mira looked at him. "For us." Arun didn't understand. "Us who?" She pointed upward. "The people of Bellweather." Arun shook his head. "What are you talking about?" Mira's expression changed. "The town isn't outside the time fracture." She looked at the walls. "We're inside it." Arun felt his heartbeat increase. "Why?" Mira's voice became quieter. "Because if the town ever leaves the fracture, everyone here dies." Arun stepped backward. "Why?" Mira looked at him for a long moment before finally saying, "Because most of the people here have already died."

Arun stared at her.

"No."

Mira didn't move.

"My father is alive."

"Yes."

"Kabir is alive."

"Yes."

"My teachers are alive."

"Yes."

"Everyone is alive."

Mira nodded. "They are alive here."

"What does that mean?"

"It means Bellweather is a copy."

Arun felt as though the ground had disappeared beneath him.

Mira explained that eighty-four years earlier, the original Bellweather had been destroyed when the time fracture expanded. Thousands of people died in an instant. Arun's grandfather discovered that the machine could preserve the town by creating a temporal copy of the moment immediately before the disaster. The machine copied everything: buildings, streets, animals, memories, and people. It created a second Bellweather inside the fracture.

"But if we're copies," Arun said, "then why do we have memories?"

"Because memories were copied too."

"And my father?"

"Copied."

"My grandfather?"

Mira looked away.

"Your grandfather was the one person who wasn't copied."

Arun opened the notebook again.

The final pages were written differently.

If you're reading this, then I'm probably gone. I need you to understand something. The people of Bellweather are not illusions. They are not ghosts. They are not machines. They are people. They laugh, they hurt, they dream, they love, and they remember. Whether they were born naturally or created by the fracture doesn't change what they are. If the machine fails, they will die because the fracture will collapse. But if the machine continues, it will eventually consume every possible tomorrow until the town has nowhere left to go.

Arun looked at Mira.

"Why are you here?"

She hesitated.

"Because I remember."

"Remember what?"

"Every version."

Arun didn't understand.

Mira explained that the machine had been failing for years. Every time it consumed a future, the timeline shifted. Most people forgot the previous versions. Mira didn't. She remembered dozens of Bellweathers that had existed before this one. In one version, the river flooded the town. In another, the mountain collapsed. In another, everyone disappeared except seven people. In another, the machine stopped working and the town simply faded away.

"And this version?" Arun asked.

Mira looked at him.

"This is the last one."

Arun looked at the calendar.

December 18.

Six days.

"What happens on the twenty-fourth?"

"The machine will attempt to remove the final possibility."

"Which is?"

Mira's eyes filled with fear.

"Tomorrow."

Arun understood.

The machine had been consuming possibilities one at a time. Eventually, there would be no future left for Bellweather. December twenty-fourth would arrive, but December twenty-fifth would not. Then the twenty-fourth would become the final day. Then the twenty-third. Then the twenty-second. Time would collapse backward until the town was trapped in a single moment forever.

"Can we escape?"

Mira shook her head.

"Can we destroy the machine?"

"No."

"Can we repair it?"

"Maybe."

Arun looked at her.

"Maybe?"

"Your grandfather left one possibility."

She opened the notebook to the final page.

The machine requires a memory to function, but it does not have to be a memory from the past.

Arun read the sentence twice.

"What does that mean?"

Mira looked directly at him.

"It needs a memory of tomorrow."

Arun frowned.

"That's impossible."

"Exactly."

"So what are we supposed to do?"

"We create one."

For the first time since entering the underground chamber, Arun felt hope.

Then he realized what Mira was saying.

"You mean we have to remember something that hasn't happened yet."

"Yes."

"How?"

Mira looked at the largest clock in the room.

"By making tomorrow happen before tomorrow arrives."

The plan sounded insane.

They would use the time fracture itself to travel a few minutes into the future, experience something, return to the present, and give the machine the memory of that experience. If it worked, the machine would no longer need to consume future possibilities. It would have a future memory to feed on instead.

But there was a problem.

The fracture only allowed one person to travel through time safely.

Arun.

His grandfather had designed the machine around Arun's bloodline.

That was why his name had been carved into the metal door.

That was why Mira had been waiting for him.

Arun didn't want to go.

He wanted to tell his father.

He wanted to call Kabir.

He wanted someone older and smarter to take responsibility.

But when they returned to the surface, everything looked normal.

The clocks showed 11:48.

Cars moved again.

Dogs barked.

The river flowed.

Nobody remembered the thirteen minutes that had disappeared.

Arun returned home and found his father sitting behind the repair counter.

His father looked up.

"You're late."

Arun stared at him.

"Did you notice anything strange tonight?"

His father smiled.

"Like what?"

Arun almost told him.

Instead, he sat beside him.

"Nothing."

His father placed a hand on his shoulder.

"You look tired."

Arun nodded.

"I am."

That night, Arun couldn't sleep.

He stared at the ceiling until morning.

At six o'clock, every clock in the town started normally.

The next day, he met Mira beneath the clock tower again.

Together, they prepared the machine.

They spent hours adjusting gears, reconnecting wires, and translating notes left by Arun's grandfather. By sunset, they were exhausted.

"You know," Arun said, "if we survive this, I'm never touching a clock again."

Mira smiled.

"That's probably a good idea."

At 11:47, they activated the machine.

The underground room shook.

Every clock began moving simultaneously.

The largest clock in the room started counting backward.

10.

9.

8.

Arun grabbed the metal railing.

7.

6.

5.

The blue light became brighter.

4.

3.

2.

1.

Everything went white.

Arun opened his eyes.

He was standing in the market square.

But something was wrong.

The town was empty.

No people.

No cars.

No birds.

Snow covered the streets.

The clock tower stood above him.

Its hands showed 6:00.

Arun looked at his phone.

December 19.

But the sun was setting.

He realized he had traveled forward one day.

Mira's voice came through the machine's radio.

"Arun, can you hear me?"

"Yes."

"Good. You have ten minutes."

"Ten minutes for what?"

"Create a memory."

Arun ran.

He didn't know what memory to create.

He wandered through the empty town.

He entered the school.

Walked through the library.

Passed his father's repair shop.

Everything was exactly where it should be.

Then he saw something that made him stop.

His father was standing inside the repair shop.

But he was transparent.

Like a reflection on water.

Arun stepped closer.

"Dad?"

His father looked up.

He couldn't see Arun.

Arun understood.

This was not actually the future.

It was a possibility.

A version of tomorrow that had not happened.

Arun walked through the shop.

His father was repairing the same pocket watch Arun had been working on when the clocks first stopped.

Arun watched him for a few seconds.

Then his father smiled.

"I knew you'd figure it out."

Arun froze.

His father couldn't see him.

But he could hear him.

"Dad?"

His father looked toward the empty room.

"You don't have much time."

Arun's eyes filled with tears.

"What do I do?"

His father smiled.

"Remember this."

The room began shaking.

The future was collapsing.

Arun reached toward him.

"I don't want to forget you."

"You won't."

The world began turning white.

His father spoke one final sentence.

"Tomorrow is worth remembering."

Arun opened his eyes beneath the clock tower.

Mira was standing over him.

"Did it work?"

Arun couldn't speak.

He nodded.

The machine was silent.

For the first time in eighty-four years, none of the clocks showed 11:47.

The largest clock in the room had changed.

Instead of the word TOMORROW, it now displayed a date.

DECEMBER 19.

Mira smiled.

"We did it."

Arun looked at the machine.

"What happens now?"

"We wait."

The next morning, Arun woke at six.

The clocks were moving.

He looked at the calendar.

December 19.

Tomorrow still existed.

December 20 arrived.

Then December 21.

Then December 22.

Nothing happened.

On December 23, the clocks stopped briefly at 11:47.

But this time, they restarted at 11:48.

December 24 came.

The entire town gathered in the market square because the clock tower had begun making a strange sound.

People whispered.

Children pointed.

Arun stood beside his father.

Mira stood on the other side.

At midnight, the clock tower struck twelve.

Everyone looked up.

Nothing happened.

Then the clock struck one.

Then two.

Then three.

Time continued.

The town had survived.

Arun looked at Mira.

She was crying.

"Why are you crying?"

She smiled.

"Because this is the first Christmas I've ever reached."

Arun didn't understand.

"How many times have you tried?"

Mira looked toward the clock tower.

"I stopped counting."

Snow began falling.

People laughed and moved through the square.

Someone started playing music.

Children ran through the snow.

For the first time, Bellweather felt completely ordinary.

And somehow, that was the most extraordinary thing Arun had ever experienced.

Years later, Arun became the new clockmaker of Bellweather.

His father's repair shop became his.

The old clock tower remained in the center of town, but the underground chamber was sealed permanently.

The clocks continued to work.

At 11:47 every night, nothing happened.

At midnight, time moved forward.

At six in the morning, the sun rose.

People forgot ordinary things.

They forgot where they left their keys.

They forgot birthdays.

They forgot conversations.

They forgot names.

But they remembered tomorrow.

Arun eventually married Mira.

They had two children.

Their children grew up believing that the strangest thing about their father was his obsession with clocks.

Arun never told them the truth.

Not because he was afraid.

Because some memories are not meant to be explained.

They are meant to be carried.

One winter evening, many years later, Arun was sitting alone in the repair shop when he noticed an old pocket watch lying on the counter.

It was the same watch he had been repairing on the night everything began.

He picked it up.

The hands moved smoothly.

11:46.

He smiled.

The second hand reached 11:47.

Nothing happened.

11:48.

Arun laughed quietly.

Then he heard footsteps.

His daughter entered the shop.

"Dad?"

"Yes?"

"Why are you smiling?"

Arun looked at the clock.

"Because tomorrow is still there."

She frowned.

"What does that mean?"

Arun closed the pocket watch and handed it to her.

"Nothing."

She looked confused.

Then she smiled.

"You're weird."

"I know."

She walked away.

Arun remained in the quiet shop.

Outside, snow began falling over Bellweather.

The clock tower stood above the town, its hands moving steadily through the night.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, behind layers of stone and steel, the old machine remained silent.

It no longer needed memories.

It no longer needed sacrifices.

It had finally learned the one thing its creators had never understood.

Time was not something that needed to be controlled.

It was not something that needed to be trapped, protected, or conquered.

Time simply needed to continue.

One second.

Then another.

Then another.

And sometimes, when the world felt uncertain and the future seemed impossible to reach, the most important thing a person could do was simply believe that tomorrow would come.

Because tomorrow was not guaranteed.

It was a possibility.

And possibilities, Arun had learned, were worth protecting.
